Chapter 3: Shadows of the Past

The boy’s mother was a distant figure, an elegant silhouette who swept in and out of the house in a whirlwind of commitments and meetings. A successful engineer, she devoted most of her time to work, building a brilliant career and a secure future for herself. He admired her intelligence and resolve, but he missed her presence—the warmth of a hug, a conversation that went beyond perfunctory questions about school and grades. He knew she loved him, but love was always second place—eclipsed by ambition and the relentless chase for professional recognition.

His father’s absence was an open wound. He barely remembered him—only fragments surfacing at odd moments: the smell of morning coffee mingled with ink—his father was a writer who always scribbled ideas in small notebooks; the rough beard scraping Gael’s cheek when he hugged him good night; the deep voice telling stories of worlds where dragons and mages lived in harmony with nature.

His father had been different from most men in Ironsight. While the city chased unbridled progress, he found beauty in simple things: a flower pushing through asphalt cracks, a bird weaving between towers, a child’s genuine smile. He turned those small observations into stories—worlds where sensitivity wasn’t weakness, but transformative force.

On a rainy afternoon—long before Gael knew anything about the Bees’ Secret—he saw his father draw hexagons in the margins of a battered notebook. His father said the world’s beauty lay in what repeated with meaning: honeycombs, wind ripples over fields, the way stories lean against each other. “If one day you hear a small song, follow it,” he whispered, as if entrusting a map—and slipped a pressed flower between the pages like a blade of sun.

He left for a “special mission”—that’s how the grandparents always called it, avoiding details. Years later, eavesdropping on hushed conversations, Gael learned his father had joined environmental activists documenting ecological crimes in conflict zones. He had promised to return soon with gifts and stories—and, above all, with proof that it was possible to make a difference in a world that seemed lost.

The promise was broken by a bomb on a dusty road in a distant country. Still, sometimes Gael felt his father was somehow present—in his sensitivity to nature, his ability to hear what others missed, his refusal to accept the world’s cruelty as inevitable. Perhaps the connection with the bees was a paternal legacy, dormant for years, waking when the world most needed it.

It was a constant shadow—but also a secret source of strength. A lack nothing could fill, and yet a reminder that some people were willing to fight for what they believed in, even at the highest price.

He watched other children with their parents—playing in the park, cheering at soccer games—and felt a stab of sadness and envy. It was like thorns in his heart. He had everything money could buy: a spacious room, brand-name clothes, the latest video games. But none of it filled the void left by his father or the emotional distance of his mother. Sometimes he wondered if it would have been better not to have been born—or if he might have been happier adopted by a family who could give him the love and attention he craved.

He felt like an intruder in his own home, a burden to grandparents who raised him out of obligation, not love. They never hugged him, never spoke words of affection, never showed genuine interest in his feelings or dreams. He retreated into his inner world, building fortresses of imagination and creativity to shield himself from the cold indifference around him. He read about distant places, exotic cultures, and fantastical creatures, dreaming of escaping Ironsight’s oppressive reality.

The discovery of the looming environmental apocalypse gave his life new meaning. He felt responsible for protecting those small beings who, like him, survived in a hostile world. Saving the bees wasn’t only about survival; it was about finding his place and building a future where love, compassion, and connection to nature were essential values. Without them, no world—good or bad—would survive.

That night, during dinner, the news vomited its routine of numbers and promises. Between commercials, the anchor smiled: “Authorities launch the Urban Disinsectization Program. New technology promises to eradicate pests in parks and squares.” The word “pests” nailed into him like a spike. His grandmother commented without taking her eyes off the screen:

“About time someone did something for this city.”

His grandfather grumbled about order and cleanliness. Gael’s stomach turned. He thought of the invisible honeycombs bees wove in silence, and the stories his father told about those who name the world so they can destroy it. It was a warning: if there was a small song begging to be followed, it would soon be silenced.

Walking home, wrapped in sadness and abandonment, a thought flashed like lightning. Despite his peculiar situation—a childhood marked by absence and solitude—he was not just another child stupefied by Ironsight’s comfortable, alienating life. He observed his schoolmates, the children of rich, successful families like his grandparents—living in privilege and consumption. They had everything money could buy—designer clothes, cutting-edge phones, exotic trips. And yet, they seemed empty, without purpose or critical awareness. They weren’t like him. They didn’t care about the future.

They didn’t care about the pollution choking the city, the social inequality ripping it apart, the ecological catastrophe threatening the planet’s future. They lived in a bubble of ignorance and indifference, concerned only with their immediate wants. He realized his loneliness and pain had made him stronger, more sensitive, more connected to reality. He had learned to value life’s simple things, to appreciate nature’s beauty, to question injustice, to fight for what he believed in.

The discovery of the impending environmental catastrophe—and the message echoing in his soul—gave him purpose, a mission that set him apart from Ironsight’s alienated masses. He was a guardian of truth, a defender of nature, a solitary warrior fighting the planet’s destruction. And even if the journey was hard, even if he felt alone and misunderstood, he knew he was on the right path. He would be the voice of resistance—the hope of a future where humanity lived in harmony with nature, averting the ecological collapse that loomed.

Walking the city’s streets, he watched the strays with compassion. Skinny dogs and bony cats wandered the sidewalks, seeking shelter and food amid the city’s indifference. They were orphans—abandoned to their fate, invisible to most. People hurried past them as if they weren’t there. Some believed “someone” took care of them—that shelters existed, that organizations were responsible. Others simply ignored them, as if they were part of the urban scenery, as insignificant as lamp posts or trash cans.

Sometimes he saw animals treated with care—given food and water by well-dressed, smiling people. But he soon realized most of those gestures were façades—a way to wash consciences and feed egos. Often they were “influencers,” using strays as props for photos and videos—displaying supposed compassion on social media to gain followers and ad money. They spent a small fraction of their fortunes on kibble and vet care while flaunting lives of luxury—indifferent to the animals’ real suffering and the city’s social inequality. He felt deep contempt for that hypocrisy, that selfish use of good as a pretext for ostentation and superficiality. He knew true compassion sought no recognition or reward—only transformation of the world into a fairer, kinder place for all living beings.

Then he saw a street cat with a deep gaze—a solitary soul curled under the willow’s shade. The cat was gray like Ironsight’s clouds, with white markings on its chest in the shape of a star. But it was the eyes that struck him—an intense green, almost hypnotic, carrying ancient wisdom, as if it had seen far more than any cat should.

It was as if the boy could feel not only the animal’s physical abandonment but also its story: once loved in a house with a garden where flowers grew freely; then the move to a smaller apartment, restrictions, financial hardship; finally abandonment—not from cruelty, but from the impossibility of keeping an animal in a world increasingly hostile to any life that wasn’t productively useful.

Their eyes met—sad yet hopeful—creating a silent connection that transcended species. It was a mute plea for help, and something more: a mutual recognition of souls that didn’t fit the world around them, that kept their sensitivity despite the hostility.

He felt a physical tug at his heart—a cord binding him to that abandoned being. He wanted to help—bring food, shelter, a home. But how? He was only a boy, with no resources of his own, no voice in the household, no power to change even his own situation.

Even so, hope sprouted in his chest like a small but stubborn flame. He ran home, heart pounding with the possibility of making a difference in at least one life. He tried again with his grandparents, this time structuring his arguments—asking for money to buy food and medicine for the animal, explaining how it could teach him responsibility and compassion.

They met him with the same sarcasm—now sharpened by irritation at being interrupted during their favorite show.

“A stray?” his grandmother scoffed. “Why would you care? It’s probably filthy, sick, dangerous.”

“Focus on your studies and stop the nonsense,” his grandfather added, voice dripping disdain.

Frustrated and discouraged, he realized he was alone in this fight. No one cared about him, no one cared about the animals. He was invisible—insignificant—a nothing amid Ironsight’s indifferent crowd. Despair swelled in him. He needed help as much as the animals he desperately wanted to save.

He needed someone to see him—to hear him—to believe him. Unconsoled, he wandered back to the square beneath the willow. As soon as they saw him, the strays gathered at his feet seeking a little affection and food—but at that moment, he wasn’t able to help.

In the dead of night, when the house finally quieted, Gael slipped into the kitchen. He took a chipped bowl and a can of sardines forgotten at the back of the cupboard. He washed the bowl in silence so as not to wake his grandparents and filled it with water. The hallway felt longer than usual; each step was a pact with himself.

The square slept under a veil of mist. The willow arched its branches like fingers over a secret. The cat emerged from the shadows—wary, but hunger defeats even ancient certainties. As it ate, the city’s noise receded and a very fine hum—almost a thought—vibrated in the air. It wasn’t the harsh buzz of Ironsight’s machines; it was a small choir, patient—like the breathing of a honeycomb.

A single bee, lost to the night, rested for an instant on the cat’s back and lifted off—as if blessing a brow. The cat purred low; Gael smiled without showing teeth, afraid to scare the magic away. When he tried to open the can wider, he cut his finger. The pain was brief. A drop of blood shone under the streetlight like newborn amber. He thought, without wanting to, that certain pains take the shape of honey.

He picked up the bowl, promising himself he’d return. If the city wanted to silence small songs, he would learn to hear them deeper still. If his grandparents wouldn’t let him help, he’d find another way—he’d ask for leftovers at the bakery, trade sticker packs for kibble, invent paths.

Ironsight, with its cold indifference, felt like a maze without exit. He carried the weight of the Bees’ Secret—and his own loneliness. He was only a boy, and yet he felt the responsibility of saving a world—even if that world didn’t care about him. And, for an instant beneath the canopy, the wind sketched hexagons in the mist—an ancient invitation, an almost inaudible answer to the map his father had left.
