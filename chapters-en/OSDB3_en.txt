Chapter 3: Shadows of the Past

Gael’s mother swept in and out like a well-tailored silhouette—present in name, absent in warmth. His father was an absence that hurt to the touch: the rasp of a beard, the smell of coffee and ink, a notebook where hexagons bloomed in the margins. “If one day you hear a small song, follow it,” his father had whispered, pressing a dried flower between pages—a sun blade left behind.

Activism took the father far from Ironsight; a roadside bomb kept him there. The gap he left became a compass. It taught Gael to treasure quiet things: a bud between cracks, a bird threading the towers, the way stories nest inside each other.

That evening the anchors smiled: a new Urban Pest Control Program, a technology to erase “pests” from parks and squares. The word hammered him. His grandparents nodded at order and cleanliness. He felt sick. If someone names a life as pest, the next step is to end it.

He walked to the willow and found a stray cat with star-white on its chest. Hunger made it brave. He fed it in the fog, cut his finger on a can, watched a drop of blood shine like newborn amber. A single bee paused on the cat’s fur, as if to bless it. Some pains, he thought, carry the shape of honey.

He carried bowls and promises home. If the city would silence small songs, he would learn to hear deeper.
