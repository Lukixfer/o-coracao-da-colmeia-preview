Chapter 2: The Bees’ Secret

The message did not come in words, but as a deep sensation, an intuitive understanding of the interconnection of all things. The boy did not hear a cry for help with his ears, but with his soul. It was as if nature itself were whispering a warning, revealing the threat of an environmental apocalypse.

He watched the bees flying among the flowers of the small garden in the square and felt a profound connection with those tiny beings. They were more than insects; they were an essential part of the web of life, responsible for pollinating plants and ensuring food production. Without them, the delicate balance of the ecosystem would collapse, unleashing consequences devastating to the planet and to humanity.

It was a plea he could not ignore. Driven by growing curiosity and genuine concern, he plunged into a world of information about those small creatures that seemed to carry the planet’s fate on their wings.

His first discoveries came from dusty books at Ironsight’s public library, an old building that resisted the city’s modernization like a last bastion of knowledge. There, between dim shelves, he found beekeeping volumes from decades past. Their yellowed pages revealed a world where bees still danced in abundance across flowering fields—a stark contrast to the present.

Online documentaries showed devastating images: empty agricultural fields where thousands of bees once thrummed, abandoned hives like ghost towns, scientists wearing grave expressions as they explained the phenomenon known as Colony Collapse Disorder. Scientific articles—written in technical language he had to decipher word by word—revealed a scenario even more alarming than he had imagined.

Bees, responsible for pollinating about 70% of the crops that feed humanity, were vanishing at a frightening rate. But it wasn’t just a numbers problem—it was as if a collective intelligence, a millennia-old wisdom encoded in each hive, were being erased from the face of the Earth.

Neonicotinoid pesticides poisoned bees’ nervous systems, leaving them disoriented and unable to find their way home. Habitat loss turned flowering meadows into concrete deserts. Climate change warped flowering cycles, creating a deadly mismatch between bees’ needs and nature’s timing. Parasites like the Varroa destructor mite spread like a plague, weakening entire colonies until their final collapse.

But there was something else—something scientists did not yet understand. In the most recent videos, Gael noticed something that chilled him: the bees seemed to be trying to communicate, forming strange patterns in flight, creating unusual geometric structures in their combs—as if they were leaving a message for anyone who knew how to read it.

And in an inexplicable way, Gael felt he was beginning to understand that language. With each page read and each image watched, a tightness grew in his chest. He began to grasp the magnitude of the problem and the devastating consequences the extinction of bees would bring. Without them, food production would collapse, leading to widespread scarcity and, eventually, the end of everything he knew and loved.

The city of Ironsight, with its indifference to nature and relentless drive for progress at any cost, represented everything broken in the world—and seemed even more terrifying now. He felt like a castaway in a sea of concrete, surrounded by people oblivious to the danger rushing closer.

One morning, as he watched the few bees that still visited the square’s improvised garden, something extraordinary happened. A solitary bee landed on his outstretched hand, and for a moment that felt endless, he felt a deep connection with the tiny creature. It wasn’t words that reached him, but images, sensations—a comprehension that transcended human language.

He saw through the bee’s compound eyes: the city as a toxic desert where flowers were rare as oases, the sky smudged gray by pollutants that confused her navigation, empty hives scattered through the suburbs like monuments to a lost civilization. He felt the despair of a queen trying to keep her colony alive as foragers returned in ever-fewer numbers.

But he saw something else too: a communication network extending beyond human comprehension, connecting all the planet’s bees into a collective consciousness sending humanity a final warning. They knew, with ancestral certainty, that collapse was close. And somehow, they had chosen him to be their messenger.

When the bee flew away, Gael stood still for several minutes, trying to process what he had experienced. He needed to do something—but what? How could a single boy face a threat so vast?

The answer formed slowly: he didn’t have to save all the world’s bees at once. He needed to start small—create a sanctuary, an example others could follow. But first, he needed to understand their language better and discover how to help them survive.

That night his dreams filled with images of blooming gardens, thriving hives, and a future where humanity had learned to live in harmony with bees. But he also saw nightmares of a silent world—no vital buzz of pollinators—where hunger spread and nature was reduced to sterile museums.

When he woke, an absolute certainty had settled into his heart: he was the keeper of a secret that could save the world—if he could share it without being silenced by the ignorance and greed that ruled Ironsight.

The journey to save the bees—and with them the planet’s future—had only begun. The urgency of it weighed on his shoulders. He needed to warn people, make them understand the gravity of the threat looming over everyone. But how to convince a city blinded by consumerism that humanity’s future depended on small insects most people ignored or feared?

He tried speaking to his grandparents, hoping that despite their coldness, they might understand. But again, his words were met with scorn and disbelief.

“Bees?” his grandmother scoffed. “Nonsense! The world won’t end because of a few bugs.”

“There are more important things to worry about,” his grandfather added, eyes fixed on the TV. “Like your studies and your future.”

Frustrated, he tried his classmates—the reaction was worse. They laughed, called him crazy, accused him of making up stories for attention. Each failed attempt to share his discovery pushed a heavier weight onto his heart. He realized that truth—no matter how important—wasn’t always welcome. People preferred to live in their bubble of comfort and ignorance, even if it meant walking toward the abyss.

He learned a bitter lesson:
Some truths are better guarded as secrets, because the world isn’t always ready to hear them.

But that didn’t mean he would give up. He would find a way to help the bees, even if he had to do it alone. The bees’ secret—and the risk of their extinction—was terrifying, and now it was the focus of his attention.

Ironsight, with its towering skyscrapers and omnipresent tech, prided itself on being a city of the future. But beneath the shining surface hid a darker reality:
It was a cult of ignorance, where truth was seen as a threat and knowledge as a privilege for the few. People lived in a bubble of comfort and entertainment, fed by media that promoted reckless consumption and the chase for instant pleasure. Education molded obedient, uncritical workers, and any challenge to the established order was quickly silenced.

With his sensitivity and pursuit of truth, he felt like a stranger in his own city. He saw the decay behind the sparkle, the ignorance behind the arrogance. And he knew the secret he carried—the truth about bees and the planet’s future—was too dangerous to share openly. He watched people around him—blank faces, lost eyes—and wondered how they could be so blind. How could they ignore the warning signs: the choking pollution, dwindling natural resources, the extinction of species?

Gael was a keeper of a secret that could save the world, but that could also destroy it. He knew he had to find a way to share the truth—but he also had to be careful. Ignorance was powerful, and those who challenged it risked being silenced. He needed allies—people willing to listen and act. But where to find them in a city that had surrendered to obscurantism?

His journey to save bees and truth grew more dangerous and lonely by the day. Ironsight was a microcosm of contemporary dystopia. Social inequality was stark—a tiny elite held wealth and power while most struggled to survive. Technology, which promised a better future, had become a tool of control and alienation. People lived under constant surveillance—cameras and drones recorded every move. Privacy was a luxury of the past; free speech, a distant dream. The culture of consumption was everywhere, urging people to seek happiness in material goods and shallow experiences. Nature was treated as a resource to be exploited; sustainability, as an obstacle to progress.

Connected to nature, he felt increasingly suffocated by that reality. He saw the hypocrisy behind the rhetoric of progress and the destruction hidden beneath the cloak of modernity. He watched the unhoused—discarded by a system that had no use for them—and wondered if that was everyone’s future. He saw youth hooked on technology, eyes glazed at glowing screens, and wondered if they could still dream of a different world.

He knew the fight to save bees was also the fight to save humanity from itself—a struggle against greed, ignorance, and indifference threatening to destroy the planet and everything living on it. He carried the bees’ secret as a heavy burden—but also as a source of hope. He felt he wasn’t entirely alone—that somewhere, people existed who shared his values and fears for the future. He was determined to find them, to build a network of resistance against obscurantism, and to fight for a world where nature and humankind could live in harmony.
