Chapter 1: Ironsight, the City of Steel

Ironsight rose like a monument to human ambition—and to its capacity for devastation. Steel-and-glass towers perforated the gray sky, competing for space that felt ever more scarce. When the sun managed to break through the thick layer of pollution, it cast a pale, sickly light over the crowded streets, revealing the dirt and decay hidden beneath the veneer of progress. The air was heavy with the smell of car exhaust, factory smoke, and piled-up trash.

People walked fast, faces tired and eyes empty, as if trapped in an endless cycle of work and consumption. Nature was a distant memory, a luxury few could afford. Parks and gardens had been replaced by parking lots and shopping malls, and the trees that still survived seemed to fight for every breath. Technology dominated every aspect of life in Ironsight. Giant screens fired off endless ads while drones buzzed above, monitoring every movement. Communication was instant but shallow, and human relationships grew colder and more distant by the day.

Amid this desolate landscape, Gael tried to find his place. He was different—a sensitive heart with a deep connection to nature that felt out of tune with Ironsight’s reality. The city oppressed him, but it also challenged him to find his own voice, to fight for what he believed in. He walked the crowded streets, trying to blend into the rushing crowd. He studied faces, searching for a sign of hope, a thread of connection—but found only indifference and fatigue. The city seemed to drain its inhabitants, turning them into automatons who lived only to work and consume.

He passed a group of teens in dark clothes and dyed hair, laughing loudly as they mocked an old man struggling to cross the street. Gael’s heart clenched, but he kept walking, afraid of becoming their next target. Farther on, he saw a young woman on a bench, her gaze lost in the void. She held a baby, who sobbed softly. He wondered about her story—what had brought her to that hollowed-out stare. He reached a square where a crowd clustered around a man speaking from a makeshift platform. He preached progress, technology, and competition, and the crowd applauded. A chill ran down Gael’s spine. The words rang empty to him.

He drifted away from the square, feeling more and more out of place in Ironsight. He knew he needed to find people who shared his values, who believed in the importance of nature and compassion. But where, in a city so cold and impersonal? As he walked, he noticed a small flyer taped to a pole: an announcement for a meeting of the Urban Gardeners Club, a group that cultivated community gardens among the concrete. A smile flickered across his face. Maybe there, among those who still believed in the beauty and power of nature, he might find what he was looking for. The discovery of the Urban Gardeners Club lit a spark of hope. He imagined a place where he could learn to grow plants, care for the soil, and connect with others who shared his love of living things—a refuge from Ironsight’s chill and the suffocating expectations of his grandparents.

When he got home after the strange encounter in the square, he found his grandparents in the living room, absorbed in a TV show about finance. The bluish glow carved shadows into their stern faces, deepening the lines of disapproval permanently etched there. The air was steeped in furniture polish and canned laughter.

“Grandma, Grandpa…” he began, a thin thread of hope in his voice, still processing the strange experience with the bee, “I found a gardening club! They plant city gardens and care for the plants, and there are bees there that—”

Before he could finish, his grandfather cut him off with a long, impatient sigh that seemed to carry decades of frustration.

“Gael, for God’s sake, you need to stop with this nonsense once and for all!” His voice climbed to compete with the TV. “Video games, gardening, bees… None of that will take you anywhere in life! A real man focuses on serious things, on real opportunities, like business and finance.”

His grandmother glanced away from the screen long enough to shoot him a look of reproach.

“Your grandfather’s right, Gael. Look around.” She gestured to the luxurious room. “We built all this by being practical, focusing on what matters: money, status, security. Your fantasies about nature are for people who don’t understand how the world really works.”

A knot formed in his throat. He knew they loved him, but they didn’t understand him. They saw him as a project to be molded, a boy who needed to be ‘fixed’ to fit their expectations.

“But Grandpa…” he tried, “I like video games—and plants. There’s nothing wrong with that.”

His grandmother cut in, disapproval sharp in her tone.

“Gael, stop acting like a girl. Video games are for children, and gardening is women’s work. A real man has to be strong, decisive, ambitious.”

Tears burned in his eyes. He didn’t fit their rigid gender boxes. He was simply Gael—with his own interests and his own way of being.

“And besides,” she added, “we don’t have money for those things. We need to save for your future so you can have a successful career and support a family.”

He looked at the expensive furniture and art around the room, then back at their impassive faces. He knew they had plenty of money, but preferred to spend it on status and appearances rather than on his dreams.

Deflated, he retreated to his bedroom—alone and misunderstood. But the spark of hope still burned in him. He wouldn’t give up on who he was, and he would find a way to connect with nature, even without their support.

Seeking refuge in the virtual world of video games, the screen lit with vibrant colors, transporting him to a realm where he could be whoever he wanted—without judgment. But even digital escape couldn’t erase the oppression that Ironsight pressed into him. The city’s cold artificiality seemed to smother his soul. He longed for contact with nature, for a place where he could breathe clean air and feel soil beneath his feet.

Looking out the window at the gray sky and the high-rises that blocked the sun, he wondered if there was any place in Ironsight where nature still held on, a green refuge in the steel maze. Then he remembered the square a few blocks away—a broad space with a few benches and trees that he rarely visited. Maybe it was a place to begin.

The next day he went to the square. He put on his jacket, laced his shoes, and left the house, ignoring his grandparents’ disapproving stares. When he arrived, he found a scene that startled him. The place he had imagined empty and calm was full of unhoused people—men and women of all ages with worn clothes and tired eyes—occupying benches and corners. Some slept, others murmured to each other; some wandered in their thoughts. Curiosity and compassion took hold of him. He had never stopped to think about their situation, about the invisibility they endured in a city obsessed with progress and individual success. He sat on an empty bench, watching. Then he noticed a small improvised garden near the roots of a large willow whose branches, nearly dry, hung over the middle of the square. A few potted herbs and colorful flowers grew there, bringing a touch of life to the desolate scene—a shock of reality. He saw an elderly woman tending the plants. She had a gentle smile and wise eyes. Who was she? Why had she created this small oasis in the middle of the square’s chaos? He gathered his courage and approached.

“Hello,” he said shyly. “Your plants are beautiful.”

She smiled. “Thank you, dear. It’s my little refuge in this place.”

“I like plants too,” he replied. “But I don’t have much space at home—not that I live somewhere small,” he corrected himself, “it’s just that they don’t let me do much… if you know what I mean.”

She didn’t know his life, couldn’t quite understand his problem—but she knew plants, and how good it was to care for them.

“Nature always finds a way to grow, even in the most unlikely places,” she said, eyes bright. “You just have to give it a little care and attention.”

As they talked, he noticed a few bees flitting between the flowers with an urgency that felt different from usual behavior. They gathered pollen and nectar, yes, but there was something else in their movements—an almost desperate resolve, as if they knew each visit might be one of the last. It was as if they had found a precious banquet in that small patch of stubborn nature, an oasis where they could still fulfill their ancestral work—not only for the hive’s survival, but for an entire ecosystem that depended on them.

He watched, fascinated, as they seemed to communicate through a complex dance, their small bodies tracing patterns in the air that looked like they carried vital information. Some flew in perfect circles, others in straight lines that shifted like a choreography. It was like observing a conversation in a language completely different from his own—and yet, in a strange way, he felt he could begin to understand.

Then an extraordinary sensation washed over him. It was as if he could hear a low hum—not with his ears, but from somewhere much deeper—a melody rising inside his mind, echoing in secret chambers of consciousness he’d never known were there.

It wasn’t just sound; it was complex—full of harmonies and patterns that resembled music, and language, and something older than both. It was as if each bee were a note in a vast symphony, and he was beginning to hear not just the notes, but the whole song they made together.

He closed his eyes and focused, heart pounding with the realization that something deeply meaningful was happening. The elderly woman kept talking about her plants, but her voice grew distant, as if he were slipping into a parallel dimension where only he and the bees existed.

And then, clear as crystal, he heard. Not human words, but communication that transcended language: images, emotions, ancestral knowledge poured straight into his mind. He saw empty hives, barren fields, a silent future where no flower would be pollinated, where life’s abundance would give way to scarcity and hunger.

But he saw something else too: a network of communication connecting all the bees on the planet, a collective intelligence that had chosen him, among billions, to receive a crucial message. A small voice that seemed to come from far away in time and space—and at the same time from inside his own heart—whispered with an urgency that raised the hair on his arms:

— Help us… time is running out… you are our only hope… keeper of the hives… awaken…

When he opened his eyes, the bees had formed a perfect circle in the air before him for a few seconds, as if confirming the communication had been real, before dispersing back to their vital work among the flowers. But something had changed in him, irreversibly—he now carried a secret that could either save or doom the world.
