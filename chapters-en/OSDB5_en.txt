Chapter 5: The Dystopia

With those simple materials, he began building a world of his own—a silent act of rebellion against the prison he found himself in. The white walls became canvases for his runaway imagination. He started by drawing lush landscapes he had seen only in documentaries and dreams: forests where centenary trees touched the clouds; vast fields of flowers where bees danced in abundance; crystalline rivers where fish swam free from pollution.

But his creations soon grew more complex. He drew fantastic creatures that seemed to rise from his deepest dreams: gentle dragons guarding secret gardens; luminous beings who healed sick plants with a golden touch; ancestral guardians whispering nature’s secrets into the ears of those pure of heart.

Between the landscapes he interwove messages of hope and resistance in colored letters: “The earth doesn’t belong to man; man belongs to the earth,” “Small acts of kindness create great change,” “Bees are guardians of the future.” Some phrases seemed to sprout on their own, as if an ancient voice whispered wisdom through his hand.

The room’s floor became a three-dimensional stage for his origami. His hands—which until then had only held game controllers—discovered a new dexterity in folding paper. Colorful birds of every known species (and some he invented) took shape: hummingbirds that seemed to hover despite being paper; majestic eagles with wings outstretched in eternal flight; small sparrows gathered in flocks as if migrating to safer places.

Delicate origami flowers sprouted in the corners: red roses symbolizing love of life; sunflowers always turning toward the light through the window; and hundreds of small simple flowers representing the humble yet essential plants that sustain ecosystems.

He also made exotic paper animals: wise elephants, loyal wolves, playful dolphins, and even tiny bees built with scraps of yellow paper striped in pen. Each paper creature carried a symbolism, a silent message about the importance of protecting all forms of life.

The room’s silence—once oppressive as a shroud—slowly transformed into a sacred space for reflection and self-discovery. He dove into his books with an intellectual hunger he had never known—exploring distant worlds and learning about humanity’s history and its relationship with nature. He discovered ancient civilizations that revered bees as divine messengers; peoples who lived in harmony with forests; cultures who considered every tree an ancestral elder.

He began to write in a journal that became more than a mere record of thoughts. Each page turned into a personal manifesto—capturing not only daily reflections but vivid dreams; visions of a better future; practical ideas to fight ecological collapse. He wrote detailed plans to create urban gardens, projects to save native bees, strategies to raise awareness about the environmental crisis.

The punishment his grandparents had conceived to break his resistance ironically became a unique opportunity for him to connect deeply with himself and crystallize his life’s mission. In forced isolation, he discovered inner resources he hadn’t imagined: boundless creativity; the ability to turn pain into art; a determination that grew with each day of confinement.

It was during those days of creative solitude that Gael began to suspect he was different in a way that went beyond sensitivity. Sometimes—when immersed in creation—he felt a subtle energy flow through his hands, giving extra life to his drawings and origami. It was as if the very act of creating with love and purpose awakened something dormant inside him—something that connected his inner world to greater forces he did not yet fully understand.

Inspired by his conversation with the woman from the square, he decided to put his ideas into practice. He wanted to make a difference—even on a small scale. And what better place to start than at home—a microcosm of Ironsight’s dystopia? He lived with his grandparents in an old, spacious house—a relic of a time when the city still had gardens and backyards. It was one of the few homes left amid the high-rises and luxury condos that dominated the skyline. The house mirrored his grandparents’ personalities: rigid, conservative, crammed with objects that telegraphed status and wealth—dark wooden furniture, Persian rugs, Chinese porcelain, oil paintings—an oppressive, outdated atmosphere.

He felt out of place there—like a bird in a gilded cage. He longed for open spaces, bright colors, a closer touch with nature. One day, while his grandmother flipped through a décor magazine, he had an idea. He grabbed an old white sheet and some colored markers and—using online tutorials—made a banner that read: “Less consumption, more life! Embrace simplicity!”

With a mischievous grin, he hung the banner in the living room window, right in front of the TV where his grandmother watched a show about celebrities and their extravagant lives. She, absorbed, noticed nothing. But his grandfather, entering the room at that moment, widened his eyes and burst into a shout of indignation.

“What is this, Gael?” he exclaimed, pointing at the banner. “Take that ridiculous thing down right now!”

Gael tried to explain—talking about runaway consumerism, social inequality, environmental destruction. But his grandfather wouldn’t hear it.

“You’re going crazy!” he yelled. “Those communist ideas will ruin your life!”

His grandmother—finally noticing—approached the window and read the banner. Her face flushed with anger.

“Gael!” she shouted. “You’re embarrassing the family! Take that thing down before the neighbors see it!”

Instead of shrinking, Gael began to laugh. Their overreaction was so absurd he couldn’t help it. He imagined the neighbors—trapped in their luxury apartments—reading the banner and wondering what had happened to the “perfect” family in the old house.

His laughter echoed through the room—but was soon silenced by their furious stares. His grandmother yanked the banner down; his grandfather gripped Gael’s arm hard.

“You’re grounded!” the grandfather barked, dragging him to his room. “No video games, no TV, no leaving the house!”

They locked him in—anger burning hot in his chest. The injustice of it consumed him. He had done nothing wrong—only expressed his opinion, his concern for the planet’s future.

The first days were the hardest. The room’s solitude and silence suffocated him. Anger and frustration bubbled in him like a pressure cooker ready to explode. He felt wronged, misunderstood. His grandparents—with their narrow worldview and materialist values—couldn’t see the importance of what he tried to do. They saw him as a rebel without a cause, an innocent dreamer who needed to be put in his place. He paced his room like a caged animal. He punched the walls, kicked the furniture, screamed until his voice broke. He wanted to smash everything—destroy the prison that held him—escape that reality.

Slowly, anger gave way to deep sadness. He felt alone—abandoned—with no one to understand or support him. He cried until he fell asleep—exhausted and desolate. The next day he woke feeling empty. The revolt had drained away, leaving a bone-deep fatigue and a sense of powerlessness. He looked at the bare walls and wondered what he was doing—what any of it meant. Then a thought sparked in him like a small flame in the dark: he couldn’t give up—couldn’t surrender to sadness and despair. He had a mission—a purpose urging him forward.

He needed to find a way to channel his revolt—transform it into something positive—something that could make a difference. Locked in his room, instead of giving in to despair, he decided to turn the situation in his favor. The room—once a space of confinement—became his creative refuge. He dug through drawers and closets, unearthing forgotten treasure: colored pencils, pens, bright papers, cardboard boxes, rolls of tape.

With those simple materials, he began to build his own world. The white walls became canvases for his imagination. He drew lush landscapes, fantastic creatures, and messages of hope and resistance. The floor turned into a stage for his origami—colorful birds, delicate flowers, exotic animals taking shape in his hands.

The room’s silence—once oppressive—became a space for reflection and self-discovery. He plunged into his books, exploring distant worlds and learning humanity’s history and relationship with nature. He wrote in his journal—recording his thoughts, dreams, and ideas for a better future. The punishment—given as a penalty—became an opportunity for him to connect with himself and his mission to fight the looming ecological collapse.

Then he remembered something important: he could still use his study computer to play.

A smile spread. It was the perfect chance to connect with others—even while grounded. The game’s virtual world was a place where he could be himself—free from judgment or expectation. There he was known for his skill, strategy, and creativity—not for a protest banner or his “crazy” ideas.

He ran to the computer, eager to log in. His fingers flew as he typed his username and password. The screen bloomed with vibrant colors and enveloping sound—transporting him to a world of fantasy and adventure. He connected to the server and was greeted by a rush of messages from friends. They were excited about newly released weapons and eager to test them in battle. He joined them—glad for the camaraderie. They talked, laughed, shared strategies, and had fun together. For a few hours, he forgot the grounding, the loneliness, and Ironsight’s dystopia. He felt part of something larger—a community of players who shared his passion and his desire to escape reality, even briefly.

As he played, a warm wave of belonging surged through him. He realized that—even in Ironsight’s suffocating dystopia—there were spaces of freedom and creativity where people connected beyond physical barriers and shared their deepest passions. And maybe—just maybe—this virtual space could be used for something more transcendent—to plant seeds of change and hope in a world that desperately needed them.

That feeling intensified when he took up his in-game role: a High Priest of Light. The choice wasn’t accidental—since creating his character he felt drawn to classes that healed and protected, that strengthened others rather than simply destroying enemies.

With mystical powers of healing, spiritual empowerment, and even resurrection, he became a central, respected figure in that community. A natural leader who inspired confidence and cooperation by action—not empty words. He guided his companions through epic battles against shadow creatures; healed wounds with golden light that flowed from his virtual hands; shielded them with barriers of pure energy; and—in the most critical moments—brought them back when they fell.

What struck him most was how others reacted to his presence. Even in a game, people felt braver—more hopeful—when he was there. Private messages of thanks arrived; requests to join tough missions; invitations to lead groups through impossible challenges.

“Dude, when you’re in the party, it feels like nothing can go wrong,” one player wrote. “It’s like you bring real light into the game.”

The words hit him like a flash of understanding. He was a symbol of hope and resilience—even in a virtual world—a beacon of light in digital darkness. If that was true in-game, perhaps it was true in real life too.

He began to see—with surprising clarity—that his mission in the real world wasn’t so different from his mission in the game. He needed to heal the planet’s wounds; strengthen the connection between people and nature; resurrect hope in hearts that had given up.

The great difference was that in the real world there is no respawn—consequences are permanent; injuries can be irreversible; time is running out. But perhaps—for that very reason—his light was all the more necessary.

He didn’t yet know how to translate his virtual abilities into the physical world—but an absolute certainty filled his heart: he was on the right path, and in some mysterious way, the universe itself was preparing him for a mission far greater than saving characters in a game.

That night his dreams were filled with images where the borders between virtual and real dissolved: he walked through digital forests turning real beneath his feet; healed injured bees with the same golden light he used in-game; led an alliance of real people who—like his virtual companions—believed that together they could make a difference.

When he woke the next morning, the punishment had lost all power over him. He had discovered who he was—and no physical prison could contain the light that had awakened within him.
